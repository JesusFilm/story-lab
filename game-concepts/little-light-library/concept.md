# Little Light Library

Status: creative concept draft for local review; name, audience and mechanics are proposals.
Created: 11 September 2026. No prototype has been built.

## The idea

A beautiful shelf of Bible storybooks that open into small, living worlds. A child
and parent choose a book, hear or read the story, play with a few meaningful moments,
and bring home a tiny keepsake that helps them tell the story again.

The invitation is simple: **open a book, discover Jesus together, carry the story
into tomorrow.** Beauty draws a family in; play gives them something to notice;
conversation helps them understand; returning to the story gives them practice
remembering. These are design intentions to test, not established learning outcomes.

This is a storybook game for shared discovery, with a proposed starting audience
of children aged 4–8 and their parents or caregivers. Adults should feel welcome
even when they are learning the Bible for the first time.

## A visit to the library

The shelf feels like a treasured corner of home: painted book covers, warm wood,
soft light and little objects from previous adventures. Selecting a cover brings
the book onto a table. It opens into an illustrated spread with gentle depth and
movement—water rippling, grass moving, a lantern glowing.

Each five-to-ten-minute visit follows a simple rhythm:

1. **Choose together.** Pick a story by its cover. A short parent note names the
   Bible passage and the question the family will explore.
2. **Listen, look and play.** Turn pages at the family's pace. Narration and readable
   text tell the story; a few deliberate interactions invite attention to its meaning.
3. **Tell it back.** Put three picture cards in order or use a small scene to retell
   what happened. Helpful prompts make trying again part of the experience.
4. **Wonder together.** Ask one open question about Jesus and one about everyday life.
   Offer an optional short prayer, with room for the family's own words.
5. **Keep a reminder.** Place a story object on the shelf. On a later visit, touching
   it invites the child to remember the story before replaying a favourite page.

Completion brings a keepsake; a perfect quiz score is never needed to receive it.
The shelf celebrates shared stories, without ranking children or measuring faith.

## First book: The Shepherd Who Finds

Begin with Jesus' parable of the lost sheep in **Luke 15:1–7**. Introduce it as a
story Jesus told, including a simple explanation of the conversation that prompted it.
The intended centre is the shepherd's care and the joy of finding the lost—not
simply a lesson that children should behave or avoid wandering.

| Spread | What the family does | What it helps them notice |
|---|---|---|
| Jesus tells a story | Open the book and listen to a short introduction. | We are hearing a parable Jesus told. |
| One sheep is missing | Look across the flock and notice the empty place. | One missing sheep matters to the shepherd. |
| The shepherd searches | Reveal a few illustrated places while following the shepherd. | The shepherd takes action to find the lost sheep. |
| Found and carried | Turn the page to the shepherd carrying the sheep home. | Rescue is the shepherd's work; the sheep is cared for. |
| A joyful return | Gather the story pictures and retell the beginning, middle and end. | Finding the lost brings joy. |
| Back at our shelf | Receive a little sheep and talk together. | The object recalls the story and its meaning. |

There is no timer, danger meter or possibility of preventing the rescue. The search
invites observation while the authored story preserves the outcome. Illustrated
search locations and touch interactions are creative staging, not details claimed
by the passage.

Possible parent prompt: “What does this story help us understand about Jesus
welcoming people?” A follow-up can gently explain repentance in age-appropriate
language rather than omitting the ending's meaning. An everyday response might be:
“Who could we help feel welcome today?” The final wording needs biblical and
age-appropriate editorial review.

## Make remembering part of the fun

Give each book one strong visual anchor, one main idea and one short, repeatable
line clearly labelled as a story summary unless it is an actual Scripture quotation.
For this book, a proposed summary is: “The shepherd finds the lost sheep and rejoices.”

Use recognition first, then gentle recall: find a picture, sequence the events,
retell with less prompting on another day. A keepsake should open a meaningful
question such as “Why is this sheep on our shelf?” rather than merely display a score.
Let parents choose whether to revisit tomorrow or later; no streak pressure.

Possible later books include **Jesus Welcomes the Children** (Mark 10:13–16),
**Peace on the Lake** (Mark 4:35–41), and **The Good Neighbour** (Luke 10:25–37).
Each needs its own story purpose and interaction. A boat could recall who Jesus is
and the disciples' response; a small travelling bag could recall the Samaritan's care.
Do not force every passage into the same hidden-object game or generic moral.

## A family experience across languages

Offer “Read to us” and “We'll read,” easy replay, large controls, captions and reduced
motion. Keep essential meaning available without sound, animation or fine motor
precision. A lightweight illustrated version could preserve the same story on devices
that cannot comfortably render the 3D presentation.

Eventually, families could switch between a home language and another language
without losing their page. Translate narration, story text, covers, questions and
parent notes together. Begin with one carefully reviewed language; a second language
is a later test, with human review of meaning, pronunciation and cultural fit.

The parent view should answer “What did we explore, and what could we talk about?”
Show books visited, the current page and optional revisit suggestions. Avoid treating
time spent or quiz performance as evidence of spiritual growth. The first prototype
can save progress on the device without child accounts or personal information.

## Editorial and visual direction

Use original storybook artwork: rich painted textures, expressive faces, readable
silhouettes and restrained motion. Represent the biblical setting thoughtfully,
including Middle Eastern people and places. Agree on how Jesus is depicted before
commissioning a whole library. The reading experience should stay clear when the
decorative effects are switched off.

Keep the Bible passage accessible to adults. Distinguish the source text, simplified
retelling and invented staging. Choose a translation with appropriate permissions
before quoting it in a product. Human editorial review should cover Scripture,
theology, age suitability and translated content. AI may assist production, but
unreviewed generated stories or answers should not reach children.

## A small first experiment

Build one complete book with six spreads, a shelf, narration, one observation
interaction, a three-picture retelling activity, a parent conversation card and one
keepsake. Prefer depth and coherence over a large catalogue. Payments, accounts,
open-ended chat and a book-generation interface are outside this first experiment.

Try it with a small group of families. Observe whether they can start unaided,
whether the child pays attention to the story as well as the effects, and whether
the conversation feels natural. Ask the child to retell the story immediately and
again on a later day, using the keepsake as a cue. Ask the parent what the child
seemed to understand about Jesus. Treat this as qualitative prototype feedback,
not proof of educational effectiveness.

The strongest signal would be a child asking to return to the book and being able
to explain something meaningful about the story. If collecting objects overwhelms
understanding, simplify the rewards before expanding the library.
